\section{Introduction}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Wheat quality assessment at FMCG procurement warehouses is performed manually. Trained technicians sort a 100-gram grain sample into twelve categories, grain by grain, with each cycle taking approximately 60 minutes \cite{Reardon2009}. This assessment is the quality gate for every incoming consignment.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{Images/100g grain sample.png}
    \caption{A typical 100-gram wheat grain sample spread on a sorting table.}
    \label{fig:100g_sample}
\end{figure}

This thesis documents the development of \textbf{``Drishti''}, an automated wheat grain analyser designed to replace this manual process. The device was developed through fieldwork at FMCG procurement sites. Every design decision was grounded in observed operational requirements.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Problem Statement}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

A 100-gram wheat sample contains approximately 2,800 grain entities. A trained technician must sort these into 12 categories based on morphology, surface colour, and kernel geometry. This skill takes months to acquire reliably. At 60 minutes per cycle, assessment becomes the rate-limiting step in a continuously operating logistics chain.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{Images/wheat categories.jpg}
    \caption{The twelve distinct grain and impurity categories identified during manual assessment.}
    \label{fig:wheat_categories}
\end{figure}

Three problems compound each other: \textit{assessment latency}, \textit{inconsistency across operators and shifts}, and \textit{concentration of tacit expertise} in a small number of senior assessors.

\section{Literature Review: The Limits of Existing Solutions}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Computer vision has been applied to grain assessment for several decades. Early systems used handcrafted feature descriptors with classical classifiers such as SVM and k-NN \cite{Lingwal2021}. These proved fragile under variable lighting and grain orientation. Convolutional neural networks improved variety-level classification substantially \cite{Laabassi2021, Yasar2024}. However, these evaluations assume sparse, well-arranged test samples. Such conditions do not exist in operational procurement.

YOLO-family detectors \cite{YOLOReview2024} applied to dense wheat-bed images produce two consistent failure modes. First, heavy mutual overlap causes prediction conflicts that non-maximum suppression handles poorly, leading to undercounts \cite{YOLODense2025}. Second, grain instances are small relative to the receptive field, and architectures such as Mask R-CNN \cite{Xu2023} struggle with dense occlusion.

A deeper problem is the assumption that a single overhead image contains sufficient classification information. For the twelve-category FMCG protocol, it does not. Diagnostic features of \textit{Infested} grain and \textit{Black Tip} discolouration are orientation-sensitive. They are invisible if the relevant surface faces downward. Multispectral approaches \cite{LiWheat2024, ElMasry2019} improve spectral contrast but do not resolve geometric occlusion.

The core constraint is physical observability, not computational power. The hardware-software co-design principle \cite{Keutzer2000} applies directly: the physical setup must be engineered to present the model with a well-posed problem. If grain orientation is varied during acquisition, the classification task becomes tractable.

\section{Research Objectives and Scope}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The aim of this research is to design, build, and evaluate an automated wheat grain quality assessment system suited to real FMCG procurement conditions. The specific objectives are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Contextual inquiry:} Map the assessment workflow and document the tacit knowledge driving category calls.
    \item \textbf{Functional constraints:} Establish physical requirements for 100-gram sample handling ($\sim$2,800 entities), dust tolerance, and a sub-five-minute cycle time.
    \item \textbf{Mechanical-computational design:} Engineer physical observability to solve the occlusion problem using a hardware-software co-design approach.
    \item \textbf{Iterative prototyping:} Build and test successive prototypes against real grain samples with expert feedback.
\end{itemize}

The scope is limited to the procurement and storage phases of the wheat supply chain, covering the twelve grain and impurity categories in standard FMCG protocols.
