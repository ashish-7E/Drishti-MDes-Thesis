\section{Introduction}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The first thing that strikes a visitor to a wheat procurement warehouse is not the scale of the operation---it is the silence of the sorting table. In the midst of arriving trucks and shouted instructions, technicians sit hunched over a 100-gram spread of wheat, working grain by grain with practiced hands. This act is the quality gate through which every consignment must pass before a major FMCG supply chain can proceed \cite{Reardon2009}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{Images/100g grain sample.png}
    \caption{A typical 100-gram wheat grain sample spread on a sorting table.}
    \label{fig:100g_sample}
\end{figure}

This thesis documents the development of the \textbf{``Drishti'' Wheat Grain Analyser}, an automated assessment device designed to address this operational bottleneck at its source. The device was developed through extensive fieldwork at FMCG procurement sites, where operational scale makes the latency problem both economically significant and technically demanding. The research was built on the conviction that good design must begin with understanding before it begins with making---a principle that shaped every technical decision that followed.

\section{Problem Statement}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

A trained technician at a wheat procurement warehouse must sort a 100-gram wheat sample into 12 distinct categories. At approximately 2,800 entities per sample, this requires close attention to morphological details, surface colour, and kernel geometry that take months to read reliably. At 60 minutes per cycle, assessment becomes the rate-limiting step in a continuously operating logistics chain. Furthermore, speed and accuracy are in structural tension, narrowing as a shift progresses.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{Images/wheat categories.jpg}
    \caption{The twelve distinct grain and impurity categories that must be identified during the manual assessment of a sample.}
    \label{fig:wheat_categories}
\end{figure}

The problem is therefore compound: it consists of \textit{assessment latency}, \textit{quality inconsistency across operators and shifts}, and a \textit{fragile concentration of tacit expertise} in a small number of senior assessors.

\section{Literature Review: The Limits of Existing Solutions}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Before describing the proposed intervention, it is necessary to examine why existing computer vision approaches are insufficient for this specific operational environment.

The application of computer vision to agricultural grain assessment has a long history. Early systems relied on handcrafted feature descriptors fed into classical classifiers (e.g., SVM, k-NN) \cite{Lingwal2021}, which proved fragile under variable lighting and grain orientation. The introduction of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) substantially improved variety-level classification \cite{Laabassi2021, Yasar2024}. However, these evaluations typically rely on a critical distributional assumption: test samples are sparse, carefully arranged, and well-lit.

When deploying YOLO-family detectors \cite{YOLOReview2024} on dense wheat-bed assessments, two dominant failure modes emerge. First, heavy mutual overlap produces prediction conflicts that non-maximum suppression handles poorly, generating undercounts \cite{YOLODense2025}. Second, target instances are extremely small relative to the local receptive field. Even architectures with stronger localization mechanisms, such as Mask R-CNN \cite{Xu2023}, struggle with dense occlusion.

Furthermore, there is a pervasive assumption that a single overhead image contains sufficient information for classification. For the twelve-category protocol used in FMCG wheat procurement, it is not. Diagnostic features of early-stage Infested grain or Black Tip discolouration are geometrically orientation-sensitive and cannot be identified if the diagnostic surface faces downward. While multispectral approaches \cite{LiWheat2024, ElMasry2019} improve spectral contrast, they do not resolve geometric occlusion.

This points to the core operational constraint: the problem is one of physical observability before it is a computational one. The underlying logic of hardware-software co-design \cite{Keutzer2000} transfers directly here: rather than asking software to compensate for physical limitations, the physical setup must be engineered to present the software with a well-posed problem. If grains can be separated and their orientation varied during acquisition, the computational task becomes substantially easier and more reliable.

\section{Research Objectives and Scope}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The overarching aim of this research is to design, build, and evaluate an automated wheat grain quality assessment system that is genuinely suited to the conditions of a real FMCG wheat procurement environment. The specific objectives are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Contextual inquiry of the procurement ecosystem:} To map the assessment workflow and document tacit knowledge driving category calls.
    \item \textbf{Derivation of functional constraints:} To establish the physical requirements for dense 100-gram sample handling ($\sim$2,800 entities), dust tolerance, and time pressure.
    \item \textbf{Design of a mechanical-computational intervention:} To engineer physical observability to solve the occlusion challenge, adopting a hardware-software co-design approach.
    \item \textbf{Iterative prototyping and validation:} To build and test successive functional prototypes against real grain samples with feedback from domain experts.
\end{itemize}

The scope is bounded to the procurement and storage phases of the wheat supply chain, focusing exclusively on the twelve grain and impurity categories specified in standard FMCG protocols.

\section{Summary}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The actual design challenge is considerably more complex than simply accelerating a sixty-minute task to five minutes. It requires understanding the limits of current machine learning models in dense, occluded environments and engineering physical observability to bridge the gap. The chapters that follow detail how that understanding was built and what it made possible.
