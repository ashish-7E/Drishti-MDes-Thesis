\section{The Necessity of Mechanical Dispersion}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

A 100-gram wheat sample contains approximately 2,800 grain entities. When loaded onto a flat surface, grains pile up and occlude each other, making reliable top-view classification impossible. Two forms of physical motion are required to address this: \textit{lateral dispersion} to reduce overlap and bring buried grains to the surface, and \textit{rotational excitation} to vary grain orientation so that defect signatures on non-top surfaces are captured in at least some frames. No optical filtering or algorithmic improvement can substitute for this; the diagnostic information must enter the imaging system physically.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Failure Analysis: Vibratory Agitation}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Vibration was the first candidate, as it is widely used in grain handling and straightforward to implement. Several vibration motor configurations were tested at different mounting positions and drive frequencies. Three failure modes were identified.

\textbf{Contact impedance:} Energy transfer from motor to grain surface was uneven. Variable coupling created spatial dead zones where grains moved minimally regardless of drive level. Higher torque worsened this, producing energetic motion at the tray periphery while the central pile remained static.

\textbf{Size-based segregation:} Prolonged vibration caused the Brazil Nut Effect \cite{Rosato1987, Ottino2000}. Denser or smaller particles---stones, earth balls, fine impurities---migrated downward, while larger fractions accumulated at the surface \cite{SizeSegregation2021}. This biased the camera view toward the upper layer, making the sample appear cleaner than it was.

\textbf{Limited rotation:} Vibration generated primarily in-plane motion. Out-of-plane grain flipping, which is required to expose non-top surfaces, was rare and uncontrollable.

\section{Electromagnetic Solenoid Actuation}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Electromagnetic solenoids deliver discrete, high-energy impulses separated by resting intervals. Each activation drives a plunger through a defined stroke in a few milliseconds, delivering a concentrated impact to the grain surface. The force profile is sharp and localised, generating upward acceleration more effective at inducing grain rotation than vibration's lateral-dominant motion.

Four solenoids were positioned at the cardinal midpoints of the tray underside to distribute vertical impulses across the full imaging area.

\section{Asynchronous Pulse Logic and Timing}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Firing all four solenoids simultaneously caused constructive and destructive wave fronts to collide, producing uneven grain motion and occasional ejection from the tray. The solution was asynchronous sequencing. Staggering solenoid activation allowed each impulse to propagate through the grain bed before the next began, producing a travelling shear wave effective for both de-clumping and rotation.

Using Raspberry Pi 5 GPIO control, with solenoids $\{S_A, S_B, S_C, S_D\}$, activation timing was structured as follows:

\begin{align}
    T_A &= t \nonumber \\
    T_B &= t + 50\,\text{ms} \nonumber \\
    T_C &= t + 100\,\text{ms} \nonumber \\
    T_D &= t + 150\,\text{ms}
    \label{eq:solenoid_timing}
\end{align}

Each solenoid deactivates 500\,ms after activation; one complete cycle spans 650\,ms. The 50\,ms inter-solenoid interval was determined empirically: below 40\,ms interference effects returned; above 80\,ms the cross-tray shear action weakened. Image capture is interleaved between cycles to accumulate orientation diversity across the full frame sequence.

\section{Hardware Integration: The Hole-Fitting Mechanism}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The mechanical interface between solenoid plungers and the sample tray is critical. Any compliance, flex, or friction at this interface converts a sharp impulse into a damped vibration, reducing de-clumping and rotation.

Two approaches were tested. The \textbf{drawer mechanism} used guide rails with magnetic retention. Magnetic retention was destabilised by the active solenoid fields, and the guide rails absorbed significant plunger energy. Dust also accumulated in the rail channels during field use, progressively increasing friction across a shift.

The \textbf{hole-fitting mechanism} became the final design. The tray has four precision apertures aligned with the solenoid plungers. On activation, plungers engage directly through the apertures, creating near-rigid force transfer with minimal damping. Loading the tray requires only a vertical placement motion. The operation takes under three seconds and involves no moving parts beyond the tray itself.

%----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Final Hardware Integration and Prototyping}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

With each subsystem validated independently, the final stage was assembly of the high-fidelity prototype. A custom PCB consolidated solenoid driver circuits and GPIO timing into a single board, replacing a breadboard arrangement that had proven unreliable under dust and vibration.
