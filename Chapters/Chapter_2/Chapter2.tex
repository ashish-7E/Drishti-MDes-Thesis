\section{Field Methodology and Site Observations}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

There is a particular kind of knowledge that cannot be obtained from a process document. It can only be observed, and even then, only if the observer is patient enough to watch the same task performed many times, under different conditions, by different people.

The fieldwork for this research was conducted over several visits to ITC procurement sites: a procurement warehouse in active operation and a downstream processing factory at which accepted grain is prepared for milling. The method followed the contextual inquiry tradition \cite{Holtzblatt1998} -researchers embedded in the workspace, observing without prescribing, asking questions as they arose naturally in the course of work rather than through structured interview protocols. The rationale is straightforward: formal interviews tend to produce accounts of how a process is supposed to work; contextual observation tends to reveal how it actually works. For design purposes, the latter is considerably more useful.

Two things were examined simultaneously at each site. The first was the physical flow of the assessment process: how grain moved from truck to sorting tray to paper register to digital application, and at which points the chain was most susceptible to delay or error. The second was the nature of the expertise being exercised, specifically, what an experienced assessor knew that a less experienced one did not, and how that knowledge was held and transmitted. The answer to that second question, as will be discussed, turned out to be more important than expected.

The warehouse and factory settings presented usefully contrasting environments. Warehouse assessment operates under conditions of external pressure: truck queues, noise, time constraints, limited lighting control, and a pervasive fine dust that settles on every surface, including grain samples in mid-sort. The factory environment is marginally more controlled, but only marginally. Both sites share the fundamental challenge of sustaining precise visual judgement across a full working shift.

\section{Stakeholders Involved }
%----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The stakeholder landscape that emerged from fieldwork was more nuanced than a conventional user-role matrix would suggest. Four distinct groups were identified, each with different relationships to the assessment process and different stakes in its outcome.

\textbf{Warehouse and factory assessors} occupy the most direct position: they perform the task. Their dominant concern is throughput---not because accuracy does not matter to them, but because the queue of trucks makes slowness immediately visible in a way that a small classification error is not. Working through a demanding shift in dusty, noisy conditions, they develop coping strategies that sometimes involve rounding judgements about borderline specimens, not through negligence but through the accumulated wisdom that not every ambiguous grain warrants the same deliberative time.

\textbf{Quality managers and administrators} sit one step removed. They interpret the results, intervene when values are close to rejection thresholds, and carry a form of institutional memory about which vendors typically supply grain at the margins and which do not. Their judgement is not purely procedural; it incorporates context that the formal protocol does not capture. One quality manager, observed at the factory site, spent several minutes cross-referencing a borderline result against a previous consignment from the same vendor before issuing a clearance---a decision informed entirely by knowledge that lived only in her head.

\textbf{Vendors and truck drivers} experience the assessment from entirely outside. For them, the outcome - accept or reject - arrives as a verdict, without explanation of the evidence or the reasoning behind it. This opacity is a real vulnerability: a vendor disputing a rejection has no way to examine the basis of the decision. Several drivers, observed waiting during periphery assessment, expressed not frustration but a kind of resigned resignation, which is perhaps more telling.

\textbf{ITC food analysts} at the Bangalore headquarters are the architects of the quality standard itself. They define the thresholds, maintain the category taxonomy, and hold the most refined version of the relevant tacit knowledge. Crucially, as Polanyi's analysis of expertise would predict \cite{Polanyi1966}, much of what these analysts know is not codified in the formal ITC documentation that warehouse assessors are trained on. The gap between what is written and what is known is a structural feature of the system, not an oversight.

\section{The ``As-Is'' Workflow: Periphery and Master Sampling}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The current workflow uses a practical two-stage logic: rapid gatekeeping first, deeper confirmation second.

\subsection{Periphery Sample Testing}

When a truck arrives, small grain portions are collected from outer \textit{boris} (gunny bags) to form a periphery sample. Three checks follow in sequence: moisture reading through a digital meter, Hectolitre measurement for variety validation (Dara, Lokwan, Sharbati, Amravati, Durum), and manual classification of a 100-gram sub-sample into twelve grain/impurity categories.

\subsection{Threshold Logic and the $W/W\%$ Calculation}

For each category, $W/W\%$ is calculated and compared with ITC thresholds. Acceptance requires impurity and defect percentages to remain below category limits. Pure wheat has no upper cap because its higher proportion indicates better lot quality.

\subsection{Master Sample and Redundancy}

If the periphery result is acceptable, unloading proceeds and a master sample is drawn from inner \textit{boris}. The complete test set is repeated to verify that inner-lot quality matches the outer-lot representation and to reduce adulteration risk.

\section{The Digital-Physical Divide}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

One observation from the fieldwork was so consistent across both sites that it warranted closer examination. Every operator maintained two records of the same assessment: one in the ITC Tab application and one in a handwritten paper register. When asked about this, the explanations were entirely pragmatic. The paper register is faster to consult during the sorting process itself---numbers can be written and glanced at without navigating a UI. It does not crash. It does not require a password to be re-entered after a screen timeout. And, perhaps most importantly, if a dispute arises about a clearance decision, the paper record is the form of evidence that all parties---assessors, managers, and vendors---have a shared convention for reading.

This pattern is not unique to ITC, nor is it a sign of backwardness. Parasuraman and colleagues' work on automation trust dynamics \cite{Parasuraman1997} describes exactly this kind of hybrid behaviour as a rational response to systems that are reliable in general but occasionally opaque in failure. The paper register is not a failure to adopt digital tools; it is a hedge against the moments when digital tools do not explain themselves.

For the design of Drishti, this observation had a direct implication: any system that required operators to abandon their paper habits entirely would face adoption resistance that no amount of accuracy improvement could overcome. The question was not how to replace the paper register but how to make the digital interface at least as intelligible at a glance.

\section{Synthesis of Design Constraints}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The four constraints described below were not derived from a literature review or from a requirements workshop. They were arrived at by sitting with the process long enough to see where it broke down.

\textbf{Latency:} Sixty minutes per sample is not a design preference; it is a physical consequence of having one person sort 2,800 grain entities by hand. The downstream effect---truck queues extending into adjacent lanes---was visible at the warehouse site. The implication for Drishti was unambiguous: the target cycle time had to be under five minutes, and anything slower than ten would provide insufficient operational benefit to justify adoption.

\textbf{Expertise concentration:} The most experienced assessors and the least experienced ones were not interchangeable in practice. Observing several back-to-back assessments made the spread visible---not in the major categories, where trained agreement was high, but in the borderline specimens: a kernel that was either \textit{Broken} or \textit{Sharbati} depending on how you read its fracture pattern; a darkened tip that was either \textit{Black Tip} or normal field discolouration depending on the assessor's experience level. Any system relying on a human operator to make those calls would inherit that variance. One that made those calls independently would not.

\textbf{Environmental consistency:} Dust, ambient lighting variability, and shift-length fatigue all act as progressive degraders of classification reliability. The pattern was subtle but visible when comparing early-shift to late-shift assessments: borderline calls became more conservative, sorting speed slowed, and re-sort rates on ambiguous specimens increased. A machine does not get tired. This is not a trivial point---it is one of the most concrete arguments for automation in this particular context.

\textbf{Adulteration detection:} Several quality managers described a specific form of quality manipulation: blending of lower-grade grain into premium-variety lots, concentrated toward the inner \textit{boris} most remote from the periphery sample draw. This is not a hypothetical concern; it is an established practice that the two-stage sampling protocol is specifically designed to catch. An automated system needs to be at least as sensitive to these patterns as the most experienced assessors, or it will become a tool that enables rather than prevents quality fraud.

\section{Summary}
%----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

This chapter has tried to do something specific: establish that the problem this thesis addresses is not simply a slow manual process that needs automation. It is a system with layered intelligence---tacit, institutional, social---that any intervention must account for rather than attempt to replace wholesale. The four design constraints that emerged from fieldwork (latency, expertise, environment, adulteration) are not requirements in the standard engineering sense; they are a description of what the real operating environment demands. The following chapters describe how Drishti was designed to meet them.
